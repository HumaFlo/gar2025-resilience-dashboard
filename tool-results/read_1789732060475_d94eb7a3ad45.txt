   179→Using probabilities to combat volatility 
   180→Looking at the past is not enough to prepare for the 
   181→future. Disaster losses vary wildly from year to year, 
   182→and that volatility can make it harder to plan. Risk 
   183→experts are, therefore, turning to tools that look at 
   184→both the past and the future by simulating thousands 
   185→of possible scenarios to help governments anticipate 
   186→impacts before they occur.
   187→29 Probabilistic models 
   188→help to do this by providing insights into average 
   189→annual losses (AAL) and probable maximum losses 
   190→(PML) and looking beyond annual fluctuations to 
   191→assess the impact of 1-in-100 or even 1-in-1000-year 
   192→low-probability and high-impact catastrophic events. 
   193→In short, such tools offer a way to see the financial 
   194→stakes of future disasters more clearly before the 
   195→worst happens.

   427→CHAPTER 6 
   428→Investing in resilience for 
   429→economic stability 
   430→There is a stark mismatch between the increasing levels of global disaster risk 
   431→detailed in previous chapters of this report and current investment in resilience. 
   432→Disasters already exert a substantial macroeconomic toll, from weather-related 
   433→events such as floods, storms, drought an d extreme heat to major hazards like 
   434→earthquakes. The toll is expected to rise sharply as such events become more 
   435→frequent and severe.
   436→1 Without urgent action to close the gap between risk and 
   437→investment, the financial and economic consequences will become increasingly 
   438→difficult to manage. 
   439→When disasters occur repeatedly, economic growth often slows and debt 
   440→increases. Developing countries, particularly small island developing states 
   441→(SIDS) and least developed countries (LDCs), face the dual challenge of higher 
   442→exposure to hazard risk and limited access to resources for risk reduction. 
   443→In such situations, it becomes increasingly expensive to insure or otherwise 
   444→transfer risk, and more money is spent on humanitarian responses when 
   445→disasters are not prevented. This high cost is not inevitable. To create a more 
   446→stable investment climate, governments, multilateral institutions, the private 
   447→sector and households must rethink and realign their investments to better 
   448→protect current and future assets. A clear, integrated risk financing approach can 
   449→help address these challenges and open a pathway toward long-term financial 
   450→and economic stability. 
   451→This chapter presents three interconnected negative feedback loops, or “spirals”, 
   452→that illustrate how disaster risk can destabilize economies, contributing to 
   453→declining incomes, rising debt, unsustainable risk transfer and repeated cycles 
   454→of response and recovery. These unsustainable development spirals feed into 
   455→one another, meaning that failure to address one can intensify vulnerabilities 
   456→elsewhere.
