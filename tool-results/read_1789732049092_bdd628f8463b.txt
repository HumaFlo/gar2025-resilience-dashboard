   258→Disaster, debt and credit ratings    
   259→Because resilience is low in many developing 
   260→countries, large-scale disasters can trigger 
   261→sovereign-rating downgrades when the need for 
   262→financial assistance is most acute. To gauge financial 
   263→risk, investors rely on sovereign credit ratings, issued 
   264→by ratings agencies, that assess a nation’s ability 
   265→to repay its debt. Major disasters can have longer-
   266→term impacts, particularly if they depress investor 
   267→confidence. Besides straining government finances, 
   268→disaster risk, if not well managed, may discourage 
   269→investment, reduce economic growth and increase 
   270→the debt. A country’s credit rating determines its 
   271→borrowing costs, influences investor confidence, and 
   272→impacts economic stability and growth. 
   273→For example, following the 2022 floods that led 
   274→to over 1,700 deaths and $30 billion in damages 
   275→in Pakistan, capital markets and the country’s 
   276→sovereign credit rating reflected substantial risk. 
   277→As climate change makes extreme events in many 
   278→regions more frequent and intense, future climate-
   279→induced rating downgrades may be more likely. 
   280→Understanding these risks is essential for wider 
   281→economies and investors because pension funds, 
   282→central banks, and insurers are all major holders of 
   283→sovereign debt.
   284→26 
   285→The fiscal landscape in many disaster-affected 
   286→countries deteriorates as governments shift 
   287→resources towards responding and recovering from 
   288→a disaster. This puts pressure on often already 
   289→stretched national budgets.  
   290→The map below, developed by the International 
   291→Institute for Applied Systems Analysis (IIASA), 
   292→shows how often a country may expect a fiscal 
   293→gap, when a disaster is so costly it can no longer 
   294→meet its debt obligations, making it more likely to 
   295→default. Concerningly, for 61 vulnerable countries, 
   296→the modelling results, which were based on a 
   297→combination of current hazard and economic 
   298→indicators, found that fiscal gaps could be expected 
   299→at least every ten years (an annual probability of 
   300→10%). In contrast, in another set of 54 countries 
   301→across low-income, emerging and advanced 
   302→economies, such fiscal gaps could be expected only 
   303→every 50 years (an annual probability of 2%), most 
   304→likely due to lower hazard vulnerability and exposure. 
   305→Averting fiscal crises in poorer countries 
   306→The IIASA study further underscores that these 
   307→economic outcomes are not inevitable. The study 
   308→shows how having better disaster risk financing 
   309→options can help stop this bifurcation between 
   310→countries increasingly caught in a rapid cycle of 
   311→disaster-related fiscal crises and those more able 
   312→to develop sustainably. Specifically, the study shows 
   313→how International Monetary Fund (IMF) Special 
   314→Drawing Rights (SDR) can soften the impact of 
   315→disasters.

   317→low-income and emerging economies were allowed 
   318→to access 10% of their SDR entitlement in case of 
   319→a major disaster. Applying this investment vehicle 
   320→delayed the likelihood of fiscal crises by 19 years 
   321→for low-income countries and 12 years for emerging 
   322→economies (a change in annual probability of 5% and 
   323→11%, respectively). This investment in risk reduction 
   324→would have a massive development dividend.

   326→urgency of explicitly making disaster risk reduction 
   327→a key principle in financial architecture reform.

   361→Using probabilities to combat volatility 
   362→Looking at the past is not enough to prepare for the 
   363→future. Disaster losses vary wildly from year to year, 
   364→and that volatility can make it harder to plan. Risk 
   365→experts are, therefore, turning to tools that look at 
   366→both the past and the future by simulating thousands 
   367→of possible scenarios to help governments anticipate 
   368→impacts before they occur.
   369→29 Probabilistic models 
   370→help to do this by providing insights into average 
   371→annual losses (AAL) and probable maximum losses 
   372→(PML) and looking beyond annual fluctuations to 
   373→assess the impact of 1-in-100 or even 1-in-1000-year 
   374→low-probability and high-impact catastrophic events. 
   375→In short, such tools offer a way to see the financial 
   376→stakes of future disasters more clearly before the 
   377→worst happens. 
   378→As outlined in more detail below, there are still gaps 
   379→in the coverage and content in this emerging field. 
   380→However, All and PML-based analytics can be a 
   381→powerful tool for policymakers, central banks and 
   382→investors to understand the human and financial 
   383→impacts of disasters on societies and economies. 
   384→Innovations in areas such as machine learning and 
   385→artificial intelligence are accelerating the pace of this 
   386→work to provide policymakers with better tools to 
   387→manage the social and fiscal risks of disasters. These 
   388→methods are also being applied to wider sectors 
   389→such as agriculture, ecosystem service losses and 
   390→migration costs. They are also being extended to 
   391→better understand hazards such as wildfires and 
   392→multi-hazard losses. These exciting developments 
   393→offer the possibility to expand significantly current 
   394→knowledge on the true scale and nature of disaster 
   395→risk, thus facilitating more targeted investment to 
   396→address these challenges.

   744→however, while resilient investments can yield
